# dreamed garden

Tagged reference: `staged-gardens` (five images, 2026-08-25)
Brand-native plates: `plates/garden-enso.png`, `plates/garden-columns.png`,
`plates/garden-specimen.png`, `plates/garden-planes.png`

Spencer's brief, verbatim: *"what does a zen garden look like when dreamed of by
an abstract digital artist? The feel of the place, but weird. Calm, but strange."*

## The one change that matters

**The garden is built, not found.**

Round one shot photographic Kyoto at night — a real place, faithfully recorded.
Every one of Spencer's five references is instead a **stage, a gallery, or an
exhibition**. Nature is plinthed, printed on screens, backlit, framed. Nobody is
pretending these are places you could walk to.

That is not a decoration change, it is a category change, and it resolves the
layer model:

> The garden was "grown." It is now **staged**. Pixel Blade does not show you the
> buyer's world — it builds a model of that world and walks you through it.
> Which is what advertising actually is.

## Mechanics

1. **Constructed, never found.** Free-standing objects in a void with no
   building around them. No ceilings. No horizon. If it reads as a location
   rather than an installation, it is wrong.

2. **Nature flattened into graphic planes.** Mountains as stacked flat cut
   shapes, each layer paler and hazier than the one in front. Trees as pure
   silhouette. **No photographic landscape texture.** This is the same
   plane-stacking as `surreal`, now applied to the garden — which is why the
   whole thing is already a parallax rig.

3. **Light is furniture.** Glowing columns, backlit discs, luminous screens.
   Light does not fall on the scene from a lantern; light *is* the scenery. This
   is the sharpest break from `night scene`, where one warm practical lights a
   real space.

4. **Mirror floor, always.** Seamless, black, perfectly still. Doubles the
   composition downward, doubles the available light, and removes any cue about
   where the viewer stands.

5. **The specimen on a plinth.** Nature presented as an exhibited object,
   floating clear of the floor, lit by a single shaft. Reverent. Slightly wrong.

6. **Tiny figures.** `scale` applies unchanged — 1–4% of frame height, turned
   away, one to three of them.

7. **Calm, never dramatic.** Nothing is happening. No weather event, no action,
   no crescendo. The strangeness is in the arrangement, never in the incident.

## The color finding

**The warm/cool split in these references is exactly bronze against petrol.**

Warm glowing objects, cool receding world. That is the same two-color engine as
the material system, and it is the real answer to "does the garden survive the
material rebuild" — it survives *because* the constructed garden and the forge
run on the same two colors. A photographic garden did not.

Notice also that the enso disc in `garden-enso.png` carries **brushed vertical
grain**: the moon is a bronze plate lit from behind. The material system and the
garden converged without being forced to.

## Borrow / don't borrow

**Borrow:** everything above.

**The coral and red are permitted — inside imagery only.** An earlier version
of this file cut them outright. Spencer overruled it with a better distinction:
they belong to the **image gamut**, never the interface. See `lit world`, which
is the governing rule. Inside a frame they are excitement and depth; in type,
tiles or text sections they are a violation.

**Do not borrow the white gallery** of the third reference. It is a light-ground
space and this brand is dark by constraint. If a bright moment is ever wanted it
has to be a deliberate inversion, declared, not an accident of reference drift.

**Do not borrow the calligraphy panels** as decoration. Same rule as `surreal`.

## Open fork

`garden-planes.png` reads as **illustration**; `garden-enso`, `garden-columns`
and `garden-specimen` read as **photographs of impossible things**. Both satisfy
the brief. They are different worlds and the site should pick one — see the
board.
